# Show the product doing what the headline promises

- ID: comp-15
- Category: Components & actions
- Tags: Hierarchy, Images, Marketing
- URL: https://axiomui.nischal.fyi/rules/comp-15

When a page makes a product claim, show the product delivering it: a crop of the real interface or output at the moment the headline describes. A reader should be able to point at the proof in the image without reading further. Decorative art that would fit any product's headline proves nothing.

- Do: Headline + deploy #418 flagged in the product
- Don't: Headline + floating avatars and a green check

## Why it works

“Find the deploy that broke checkout” is a claim a visitor can check. A crop of the deploy list with #418 flagged beside a 38% rise in checkout errors lets them see the product keep the promise before reading a word of copy. Eye-tracking studies by Nielsen Norman Group found that people study images that carry information, such as real products and real people, and skip generic decorative imagery.

- [Nielsen Norman Group · Photos as web content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)

## What breaks

Floating avatars, a green check and soft blobs would sit just as well under a competitor’s headline, so the most prominent element on the page tells the visitor nothing. They have to take the claim on trust or scroll to find out what the product looks like, and a skeptical evaluator often leaves first.

## How to apply it

1. Choose the frame from the headline: the screen and state where the promised result is visible. Use real or realistic data, then crop and enlarge until the evidence is readable at the size the image ships.
2. Keep the proof legible without hover or animation. Mark the result with the product’s own highlight, and write alt text that states what the image shows, such as “Deploy 418 flagged as the cause of a 38% rise in checkout errors”.
3. To find violations, open each hero and feature section and list what the image depicts. Flag images whose files or components are named like `blob`, `gradient`, `avatars`, `illustration` or `abstract`, and heroes where no element of the product’s interface or output appears. Alt text that is empty or generic, such as “dashboard” or “team”, is a second signal.

## Review questions

1. Cover the headline. What does the image alone show the product doing?
2. Swap a competitor’s name into the headline. Does the image still fit? If so, it proves nothing.
3. Point to the exact spot in the image that delivers the headline’s promise. Can someone else find it at the size the image ships?
